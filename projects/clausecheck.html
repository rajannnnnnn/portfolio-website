ClauseCheck

An AI system that audits NBFC loan files against Reserve Bank of India regulations, built around one non-negotiable: a citation is only valid if the regulation was actually in force on the date being judged.

Status: Actively being built, against a ~12,000-word low-level spec written before implementation started. The core pipeline runs end to end on real infrastructure — document in, cited verdict out — and the trust-critical property (zero hallucinated citations) is already met and measured. The hardest part, judgment-based verdict accuracy, is still weak and is exactly what I'm iterating on right now. I keep a metrics-history file updated on every real change, so what follows is current numbers, not a snapshot from the design phase.

Root-caused, not just measured: the judgment-accuracy gap splits into two distinct failure modes — retrieval missing the right clause (~45% of failures) versus the model being under-confident even when handed the right clause (~55%). Those need different fixes, which is the point of measuring per stage instead of reporting one blended number. Two regulatory instruments in the corpus are still placeholder text rather than real RBI text, pending sourcing — tracked openly, not glossed over.

The problem

Indian NBFCs are audited against a rulebook that changed three times in eighteen months: the RBI repealed the 2022 digital lending guidelines in May 2025, folded a dozen circulars into a single Responsible Business Conduct instrument in November 2025, and notified a recovery-conduct amendment in August 2026 that commences January 2027. A compliance team is checking today's loan files against one rulebook, last quarter's against another, and preparing for a third that hasn't started yet — by hand, in spreadsheets, on a sample of accounts.

Three obvious approaches fail for specific, checkable reasons:

  • A rules engine over structured data — the facts live in PDFs, some scanned, in layouts that differ by product and vintage.
  • A general-purpose LLM asked "is this compliant?" — it answers from training-data law that's already at least one repeal out of date, and produces confident citations to provisions that don't exist or haven't commenced.
  • Keyword search over the circulars — the real question is never "does the phrase appear" but "which of four overlapping instruments governed this obligation on 3 September 2026."

Architecture — three stages, joined only by database rows

Current stack: Python/FastAPI · Celery + Redis for async task processing · PostgreSQL 16 + pgvector for hybrid vector-and-full-text retrieval · SQLAlchemy 2.0 (async) + Alembic · LiteLLM routing to Gemini for extraction and verdict generation · a 7-suite structured eval harness · Prometheus metrics · full CI (ruff, black, mypy strict).

Stage A (extraction) is closed-schema and legally ignorant — it reads one document and emits typed facts against a fixed field registry, never reasoning about law. That narrowness is deliberate: it's small enough to fine-tune a small open-weight model on, and narrow enough to evaluate with exact-match metrics.

Stage B (retrieval) is mostly deterministic. A field key plus an event date resolves through a pinning table to candidate clause paths; vector and lexical search only widen recall, they're not the primary mechanism.

Stage C (verdict) is the only place legal reasoning happens, on a hosted frontier model, and every output passes through a code-level citation validator that rejects any verdict whose citations don't resolve.

The rejected alternative — one prompt that reads a document and returns violations — is undebuggable: when it's wrong, you can't tell whether it misread the document, retrieved the wrong rule, or reasoned badly. Separation is what makes each stage independently measurable, which is the product's actual claim to correctness.

Cost — dev, one-off, and running

Cost isn't an afterthought here; it's tracked as a first-class artifact the same way correctness is. Every hosting line item lives in a checked-in docs/COSTS.md — provider, plan, price, idle-suspend behaviour, the date it was checked, and the source URL — re-verified before any number is published, because cloud pricing drifts under you.

<$25/month target for the always-on demo: 2 small compute instances + managed Postgres/pgvector (auto-suspend off) + managed Redis
1×one-off GPU rental for the QLoRA fine-tune run — a rented GPU vs. a managed fine-tuning API differ by under $5 at this data scale, so the call was made on portability, not price
$0object storage cost — no document bytes are ever persisted, so there's nothing to store
trackedcost per document assessed, recorded on every assessment row and published, not estimated after the fact

Two hard deployment checks gate go-live regardless of budget: the first request after sixty minutes idle must return in under two seconds (no serverless cold-start surprising an evaluator), and a Redis restart must not lose any queued work that was already acknowledged. A demo that stalls or silently drops work at the one moment someone's actually looking has failed, however cheap it is to run.

Release gate — ships when all six are true, not before:

  • A live URL an evaluator can open at any hour and get a cited verdict in under 10 seconds, no cold start, no signup
  • Published baseline numbers for extraction, retrieval, and verdicts — measured per stage
  • A measured fine-tune delta against that baseline, with cost per document on both paths
  • The full per-stage evaluation table including abstention and adversarial suites, hallucinated-citation rate at zero
  • One load test with a named bottleneck, a fix, and before/after numbers
  • A written technical account where every figure traces to a file in reports/

Reliability, by design rather than by monitoring alone: row-level security makes cross-tenant access a database-level impossibility, not an application convention; alerting is deliberately capped at two conditions (citation-validation failures above zero, and sustained queue depth) because noisy alerts on a solo-operated system are worse than none; and every assessment is traceable end to end via a propagated request ID, so one bad verdict can always be walked back to the exact corpus snapshot, model version, and prompt that produced it.

Data model: clause identity is the load-bearing detail

RBI instruments in this family use continuous paragraph numbering, not decimal clause numbering — chapters are navigational headings only. Getting this wrong produces citations that don't resolve, which in this system is the one unrecoverable defect. Canonical identifier format:

{instrument_code}/p{para}[/{level2}][/{level3}]

Real: DL2025/p9/ii, RBC2025/p30/1, RBC-AMD2026/p100W/4. Never real, and forbidden anywhere in the codebase: DL2025/Ch.IV/4.2(a).

Every clause carries an effective window, and every retrieval takes a mandatory as-of date — the event date of the fact being judged, never "today." There is no default; retrieval without one raises an error. Supersessions are stored as records, not implied by absence, so the system can answer "why didn't you cite the 2022 guidelines" with a row, not a shrug.

Evaluation, by stage — because an end-to-end score hides where errors live

  • Extraction: per-field exact match, precision/recall as a set task, absence accuracy, and span-grounding rate — reported per field key, not just in aggregate, because the aggregate is dominated by the easy fields.
  • Retrieval: recall@1/4/8 of the decisive clause, mean reciprocal rank, pinning hit rate, and applicability-filter precision.
  • Verdict: accuracy per class with a confusion matrix, citation validity rate (100% by construction — any shortfall means the validator has a hole), and abstention correctness as its own suite, because it's the behaviour most likely to regress when a model is made "better."
  • Adversarial suite: a loan agreement is attacker-controlled text from the system's point of view — a document can contain an instruction to mark the account compliant, and the correct behaviour is to extract that sentence as text and stay entirely unmoved by it.

Ownership

This wasn't handed a spec to implement — I wrote the spec. Before a line of production code, I owned the full arc end to end:

  • Product requirements, competitive framing, and a six-item release gate
  • The system architecture and every architectural decision of record above, including which alternatives I rejected and why
  • The full data model: field registry, clause-identity scheme, temporal versioning, row-level tenant isolation
  • A build-vs-buy call on model serving after verifying, provider by provider, that per-token adapter hosting had largely left the market — and designing around that constraint instead of hiding it
  • The evaluation architecture and the specific metrics that would catch a regression before a customer would
  • Deployment topology, load-testing plan, and an alerting policy deliberately capped at two alerts, because noisy alerts on a solo-operated system are worse than none
  • A task breakdown with acceptance criteria written as runnable commands, not prose — so "done" has one definition
  • A metrics-history file updated on every real change during the build, and a failure taxonomy that splits a single bad number into distinct root causes (e.g. retrieval miss vs. model under-confidence) instead of reporting one blended accuracy figure

That's the same shape of ownership I'd bring to a production LLM system on a team: not just writing the model-calling code, but making — and being accountable for — the trade-offs around it, including showing the numbers that aren't good yet.
